Picture a Saturday morning: four players are booked for 8:20. Two arrive. Their friends couldn't make it, and nobody thought to call.

The person at the counter has to decide what happens next. Charge for four? Take payment for two? Call the manager while the next group waits?

Those decisions are easier when the course has agreed on a policy before Saturday morning. But the work starts earlier, with how golfers book and what you ask them to do when their plans change.

## Give the person booking a clear instruction

One person makes the reservation. The other three may never see the confirmation email or read the booking rules. Write the instructions for the person whose name is on the tee sheet.

Tell them how to change the player count, when they need to do it, and what your policy says about players who don't arrive. Put that information where they can see it before completing the booking, then repeat it in the confirmation.

If changes must be made by phone, include the number and the hours someone will answer. "Contact the course" leaves them to look up details you could have provided in the same sentence.

Read the message on a phone. If the useful part sits below a large logo, several promotions, and a paragraph welcoming them to the course, move it up.

## Make room for a group to shrink

A golfer who wants to change four players to three shouldn't have to guess whether cancelling the whole reservation is the only option.

Check what your booking system actually lets them do. If they can edit the player count, show them where. If they can't, give them a simple way to reach staff and make sure whoever answers knows how to update the reservation.

You can add a plain reminder to the confirmation:

> If someone in your group can't make it, please let the pro shop know as soon as you can so we can update your reservation.

Follow that with your actual contact details and policy. Don't imply that a late change is free if it isn't.

Test the process yourself. Ask someone unfamiliar with the system to find the instructions and explain what they would do. If they can't tell, revise the wording before adding another reminder email.

## Decide what staff can handle themselves

A policy still leaves room for questions. What if the golfer says they called yesterday? What if staff wrote down the change but didn't update the tee sheet? What if the missing player is parking the car?

Work through those situations before putting someone on the counter alone. Staff should know where to look for reservation notes, what they can resolve themselves, and when they need a manager.

Keep a short reference beside the desk. Include the current policy, the person who can approve an exception, and where to record the decision. If you allow discretion for a first occurrence, explain what that means so the answer doesn't depend entirely on who happens to be working.

Any charge should follow the terms the golfer agreed to and your payment setup. A busy employee shouldn't be inventing a fee while a group waits to pay.

## Keep the conversation factual

Give staff language they can use without sounding like they're reading a warning notice. For example:

> "I have this booked for four. Are the other two still coming, or should I update the group to two?"

That establishes what's happening before anyone gets into the policy. If the golfer says they already called, check the notes. If there is a fee under the agreed policy, explain it calmly and bring in the manager when required.

Record any exception so the next employee has the same information. Keep the note brief and factual: what changed, who approved it, and what the golfer was told. Speculation about why someone missed a round won't help the next shift.

## Count missing players separately

A group that arrives two players short and a group that never arrives leave you with different situations to manage. Track them separately, even if you start with a basic spreadsheet.

Record the tee time, players booked, players checked in, and whether anyone gave notice. Also note whether the open spots were filled. That last detail keeps you from treating every missing player as the same financial outcome.

Review a few weeks of entries before changing the policy again. Are short groups concentrated around a particular day? Are golfers trying to make changes when the shop is closed? Are the same booking instructions causing confusion?

Repeated problems tied to one reservation holder may need a direct conversation. Problems spread across many golfers are a reason to recheck your instructions and change process first.

## Try it with the next reservation

Start by checking one confirmation email and agreeing on one set of instructions for the desk. Have staff use them, write down the questions they still get, and adjust from there.

Keep those notes for your [end-of-season review](/blog/golf-course-offseason-review), too. They'll be more useful than trying to remember which Saturdays went sideways.
