Before everyone heads out for the winter, ask your pro shop staff one question: What did you have to explain over and over this year?

Start there. If golfers kept calling about the same booking rule, look at how it appears on your website and in the confirmation email. If only one person knew how to handle league changes, have them write down the process before they leave.

You don't need a full business review to get something useful out of this. Set aside half an hour while the season is still fresh. Bring a notebook. Ask the people who worked the counter, including the weekend staff you might not see as often.

## Ask for the last time it happened

"The booking system is frustrating" gives you very little to work with. Ask what happened the last time someone got stuck.

Was a golfer trying to remove one player from a reservation? Did the staff have to enter the same information twice? Was a rate correct on the tee sheet but different on the website?

Write down the actual steps. Who noticed the problem, what they tried, and who eventually sorted it out. Leave names out if that makes people more comfortable talking.

Keep an ear out for phrases like "I always just call Sarah" or "we keep that in a separate spreadsheet." Those are worth a follow-up question. There may be a good reason for the workaround. You still need to know how it works when Sarah takes a day off.

## Check the questions golfers kept asking

Make a short list of the calls that interrupted the counter most often. You could start with these prompts:

- Did golfers know whether their rate included a cart?
- Could they find the cancellation deadline before booking?
- Did they know how to change the number of players?
- Were league players clear on where and when to check in?
- Did weather updates reach people before they drove to the course?

Pick a question from your own list and follow the booking process on your phone. Read the rate description, make a test reservation if your setup allows it, and look at the confirmation.

If you can't find the answer without hunting for it, rewrite that part. A clear sentence beside the booking button may be enough. Remember to update the confirmation too, so golfers don't receive two different explanations.

For groups arriving with fewer players than booked, we've put together a separate guide on [handling short groups and no-shows](/blog/foursome-shows-up-with-two-players).

## Get the league details out of someone's head

Before your league coordinator wraps up, ask them to walk through an ordinary league night and then a rained-out one.

Where is the current roster? Who approves substitutes? Who collects payment? What happens when a group wants to move its start time? Which arrangements were agreed to over the phone and never written down?

Put those answers somewhere the people covering the desk can find them. A shared document is fine. Include the coordinator's contact details, the agreed schedule, and any exceptions staff need to know about.

Then have another employee read it. If they still need to call the coordinator to understand the instructions, fill in the missing part together.

## Save examples before you forget the details

A note that says "fix reports" won't mean much in February. Save the report you couldn't use, with a sentence explaining what you needed from it.

Do the same for confusing confirmation emails, duplicate reservations, and rate mismatches. Keep these records in a staff-only location and leave out payment details or other sensitive information that isn't needed to explain the issue.

A useful note might read: "We needed to see paid league rounds separately from public rounds, but this report combined them. Jamie spent Friday morning splitting them out."

Now you have something specific to check with your software provider. You can ask whether a setting, a different report, or a change to your process would solve it.

If you do decide to replace the system, those examples also make a much better demo checklist. Our [guide to switching tee sheet software](/blog/how-to-switch-tee-sheet-software) covers the preparation involved.

## Choose what you'll actually finish

Once the list is on paper, resist turning every item into a winter project. Choose two or three that caused repeated trouble and that someone has time to own.

For each one, write down a person, a due date, and what finished looks like. "Improve golfer communication" is too loose. "Update the cart information on the booking page and confirmation email by February 1" is something you can check.

Leave the rest of the list intact. A lower-priority problem doesn't need to disappear just because you aren't fixing it this winter.

Before opening, ask a returning employee to try the new process without coaching. Have them change a reservation, find the league instructions, or explain the cart policy using what's on the screen. Anything they have to guess at still needs work.
